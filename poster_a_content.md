# Federated Anomaly Detection Across ARM and x86 Edge Devices: Characterizing the Heterogeneity Crossover

## Abstract

Edge devices deployed for anomaly detection, sensors, gateways, cameras, drone payloads, are increasingly expected to run intelligence locally rather than relying on continuous cloud connectivity. This shift is driven by three compounding constraints: limited on-device compute, unreliable or expensive bandwidth, and a growing requirement that raw operational data never leave the device on which it was generated. Federated learning (FL) is a natural architectural fit for all three constraints simultaneously, since it trains a shared model by exchanging only model weight updates rather than raw data, and the resulting models can be made small enough to run inference in well under a millisecond on commodity edge hardware. However, the practical benefit of federation over simpler centralized or local-only training is not uniform, it depends heavily on how heterogeneous the participating devices' data distributions are, and this dependency is rarely characterized using genuinely different hardware. Most published federated anomaly detection systems either deploy identical device fleets or simulate heterogeneity by artificially relabeling a single dataset. In this work, we federate a compact multilayer perceptron across two physically and architecturally distinct edge devices, an ARM64 Raspberry Pi 4 and an x86_64 gateway machine, whose differing instruction sets, thermal behavior, and frequency-scaling characteristics produce naturally non-identical telemetry distributions. We complement this real-hardware deployment with a controlled simulation sweeping the degree of statistical heterogeneity across ten simulated clients and five random seeds, allowing us to both observe a real crossover and explain its location theoretically. Our results show that federation outperforms both centralized and local-only training across the realistic-to-high heterogeneity regime, achieving an F1 score of 0.937 with AUROC of 0.991 on real hardware at a total communication cost of approximately 290 kilobytes across twenty federation rounds, comparable to transmitting fewer than six JPEG thumbnails, and well within the budget of a single 2G or EDGE-class mobile data session. Critically, we also identify and characterize the point at which this advantage disappears: under extreme non-identical data distributions, federated averaging suffers a well-understood form of client drift and is outperformed by simple centralized pooling. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to localize this crossover point using telemetry collected from genuinely heterogeneous instruction-set architectures rather than synthetically partitioned data, offering edge-system designers a concrete operating envelope within which federation can be trusted to help rather than hurt.

## Introduction

The deployment of machine learning at the network edge has moved from a research curiosity to an operational necessity across a growing range of applications, including industrial monitoring, agricultural sensing, perimeter security, and autonomous robotics. Each of these domains shares a common set of constraints that distinguishes edge deployment from conventional cloud-centric machine learning. First, edge devices are compute-constrained almost by definition: the hardware budget for a sensor node, a gateway, or a drone payload is typically an ARM-class processor with a handful of gigabytes of RAM, ruling out the large neural architectures common in cloud inference and demanding instead models with parameter counts in the hundreds rather than millions. Second, edge deployments are frequently bandwidth-constrained, operating over 2G or EDGE cellular links, satellite backhaul, or intermittent Wi-Fi in environments where continuous high-throughput connectivity to a central server simply does not exist; streaming raw sensor telemetry to the cloud for processing is, in many of these settings, not merely inefficient but practically infeasible. Third, an increasing number of deployment contexts impose strict data sovereignty requirements, where raw operational data, particularly from surveillance, security, or healthcare-adjacent sensing, must remain on the device that generated it for regulatory, contractual, or trust reasons, precluding centralized data collection entirely. Federated learning was conceived precisely to address constraints of this kind: rather than transmitting raw data to a central server, each participating device trains a local copy of a shared model on its own data and transmits only the resulting model parameters, which a coordinating server then aggregates, typically via simple weighted averaging, into an improved global model that is redistributed for the next round of local training. This architecture satisfies the bandwidth constraint because model updates are orders of magnitude smaller than raw data, satisfies the sovereignty constraint because raw data never leaves the device, and is compatible with the compute constraint provided the underlying model is kept appropriately small. What is less well understood, and is the central concern of this work, is whether and when this collaborative training procedure actually outperforms the simpler alternatives of training each device independently or, where permitted, pooling all data centrally, a question whose answer depends critically on how different the participating devices' underlying data distributions are from one another, a property we refer to throughout as data heterogeneity.

## Related Work

Federated learning for anomaly and intrusion detection on edge and Internet-of-Things hardware is now a substantial and active area of research rather than an open or unexplored problem, and it is important to position this work honestly within that landscape. The FedIoT framework demonstrates that FedAvg-based attack detection can be trained efficiently across a network of real Raspberry Pi devices, showing that both end-to-end training time and memory overhead remain affordable on resource-constrained hardware while achieving strong detection performance across a range of attack types. Tiny Federated Learning extends this line of work to even smaller microcontroller-class devices, achieving an F1 score of approximately 97 percent for on-device anomaly detection on ESP32-based industrial nodes operating over genuinely constrained networks, with a follow-up transfer-learning variant further reducing communication and computation overhead while maintaining comparable accuracy. At the opposite end of the hardware spectrum, a federated long short-term memory autoencoder applied to real telemetry from the Marconi100 Tier-0 supercomputer improves average F1 score from 0.388 to 0.867 through federated transfer learning across one hundred compute nodes, demonstrating that the underlying principle scales from microcontrollers to high-performance computing infrastructure. Closer to our own hardware setting, a smart-grid-focused study constructs a Raspberry Pi-based testbed and benchmarks federated learning feasibility directly through measured CPU, memory, power, and bandwidth consumption across seven different machine learning models, in both centralized and federated configurations. Taken together, these works establish convincingly that federated anomaly detection is both technically feasible and practically beneficial on constrained edge hardware, and a 2025 systematic review in ACM Computing Surveys further confirms that the field has matured to the point of supporting comprehensive literature synthesis. What remains comparatively unexplored, however, is the specific question of how the degree of data heterogeneity between participating devices governs the magnitude and even the sign of federation's benefit, particularly when that heterogeneity arises organically from genuinely different hardware architectures, differing instruction sets, thermal envelopes, and frequency-scaling behavior, rather than from synthetic partitioning of a single homogeneous dataset, which is the predominant experimental methodology in the literature surveyed above. This gap is the specific contribution this work targets.

## Research Question

Under what degree of statistical heterogeneity in client data distributions does federated learning outperform both centralized training, in which all data is pooled into a single model, and local-only training, in which each device learns exclusively from its own observations, when the task is anomaly detection on system-level telemetry collected from low-compute edge hardware? And critically, does this relationship, observed and quantified through controlled, simulated partitioning of a heterogeneity parameter, hold when the heterogeneity in question arises not from artificial relabeling of a single dataset but from genuinely different underlying hardware architectures, specifically an ARM64 embedded device and an x86_64 gateway machine, whose differing instruction sets, power management behavior, and input-output characteristics naturally produce non-identical telemetry distributions without any explicit partitioning scheme being imposed by the experimenter? We hypothesize, consistent with established federated learning theory, that federation will provide substantial benefit over both alternatives across a broad range of realistic heterogeneity levels, but that this benefit will not be unconditional: at sufficiently extreme levels of non-identical data distribution across clients, the simple weighted-averaging procedure underlying the standard FedAvg algorithm is expected, on theoretical grounds, to suffer from a phenomenon known as client drift, in which locally optimal model updates diverge so substantially from one another that their naive average is a poor approximation to any client's true optimum, ultimately degrading global model performance below what simple centralized pooling would achieve. Locating this crossover point precisely, and confirming that it manifests not only under controlled simulation but also under the milder, organically-arising heterogeneity present in real cross-architecture edge deployments, constitutes the practical and scientific core of this investigation, and is intended to give edge-system designers a concrete, evidence-based operating envelope within which they can decide whether federated training is the appropriate architectural choice for their particular deployment, rather than relying on the often-repeated but insufficiently qualified claim that federation is simply and unconditionally superior to its alternatives.

## Method

Our experimental design separates two complementary questions that are conflated in much of the prior literature: how performance depends on the controlled, quantified degree of data heterogeneity, and whether real physical hardware heterogeneity produces effects consistent with that controlled study. For the underlying anomaly detection task, we use a deliberately compact multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 32 and 16 units respectively, ReLU activations, and a final binary classification layer, totaling approximately 700 trainable parameters and occupying under 10 kilobytes on disk, chosen specifically to respect the inference and storage budget of Raspberry Pi-class hardware, with measured inference latency under one millisecond per sample. For the real-hardware component, our two participating devices are a Raspberry Pi 4 with 4 gigabytes of RAM running an ARM64 Linux kernel, and an x86_64 gateway machine; these differ not only in instruction set architecture but in thermal envelope, dynamic frequency scaling behavior under load, and input-output subsystem characteristics, all of which manifest as systematically different distributions over the system telemetry features we collect, without any explicit heterogeneity injection on our part. For the controlled simulation component, we partition a pooled telemetry dataset across ten simulated clients using a Dirichlet distribution with concentration parameter alpha, following the now-standard methodology for inducing controllable non-identical data distributions in federated learning research; large values of alpha approximate the independent and identically distributed case in which every client sees a similar mixture of normal and anomalous samples, while progressively smaller values concentrate each client's data increasingly around a single class, approximating extreme heterogeneity. We sweep alpha across the values 100, 10, 1, 0.5, and 0.1 to span this full range. At every heterogeneity level and on the real hardware, we compare three training regimes: local-only training, in which each client trains exclusively on its own partition with no communication whatsoever; centralized training, in which all available data is pooled into a single model, serving as a heterogeneity-independent reference point since it does not depend on how data happens to be distributed across clients; and federated training using the standard FedAvg algorithm, in which each client performs five local epochs of training per communication round before transmitting its updated weights for sample-size-weighted averaging at the server, repeated across twenty total rounds. Every controlled-simulation configuration is repeated across five independent random seeds, and we report the mean and standard deviation of both F1 score and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, evaluated throughout on a single held-out test set common to all three training regimes to ensure strict comparability.

---

## Dataset

The telemetry dataset underlying both the real-hardware experiment and the pooled data used for controlled simulation was collected directly from both participating physical devices at a sampling rate of one observation per second, capturing eleven system-level features per sample: CPU utilization percentage, CPU clock frequency in megahertz, one-minute and five-minute load averages, memory utilization percentage and absolute memory used in megabytes, swap utilization percentage, disk read and write throughput in kilobytes per second, and network bytes sent and received per second. Critically, and in contrast to an earlier iteration of this data collection in which anomalies on one device were injected using a real stress-testing tool while the second device's anomalies were synthetically generated to approximate similar conditions, the dataset used in the results reported here employs identical real anomaly injection methodology on both devices, using the stress-ng load-generation utility configured identically on the Raspberry Pi and the gateway machine, eliminating a confound in which apparent differences between devices could have been partially attributable to differences in anomaly generation methodology rather than genuine hardware heterogeneity. The anomaly injection schedule rotates through five distinct stress categories, sustained CPU load spikes, memory pressure induced through allocation of a substantial fraction of available virtual memory, input-output storms generated through concurrent disk and filesystem stress workers, disk thrashing through large sequential read-write operations, and CPU cache pressure workloads, with each category's duration and the intervening normal-operation periods randomized within bounded ranges to avoid producing an artificially periodic signal that a model could exploit without learning genuine anomaly characteristics. Normal-operation periods themselves are not uniformly idle but include a probabilistic admixture of light background CPU load, intended to produce a more realistic baseline against which anomalies must be distinguished, rather than the artificially pristine normal-operation periods that can make anomaly detection appear easier than it would be in a genuinely deployed system. Data collection was extended to approximately four hours per device, yielding a substantially larger sample than our initial forty-minute pilot collection, and each observation is labeled both with a binary anomaly indicator and with a categorical anomaly-type label identifying which, if any, of the five stress categories was active at the time of observation, enabling future per-anomaly-type analysis beyond the binary detection task reported in this poster.

## Experiments

We conduct two complementary experiments designed to triangulate the same underlying phenomenon from two different angles, each compensating for a specific limitation of the other. The first experiment is a controlled heterogeneity sweep, in which we take the full pooled telemetry dataset from both devices and partition it synthetically across ten simulated clients at five distinct levels of statistical heterogeneity governed by the Dirichlet concentration parameter described in the Method section, training and evaluating all three comparison regimes, local-only, centralized, and federated, at every heterogeneity level across five independent random seeds, for a total of seventy-five distinct training runs for the federated and local conditions combined, plus five additional centralized runs that serve as a heterogeneity-independent reference. This experiment's primary strength is statistical: by controlling the heterogeneity parameter directly and replicating across multiple random seeds, we can produce a clean, quantified relationship between heterogeneity and relative model performance, complete with variance estimates that allow us to distinguish genuine effects from noise, and by scaling to ten clients rather than the two physical devices we have available, we satisfy the methodological expectation in the federated learning literature that meaningful claims about federation's behavior be demonstrated across a client population large enough to approximate realistic deployment scale rather than a minimal two-party setting. Its primary limitation is that the heterogeneity it induces, while methodologically standard, is nonetheless artificial, it is produced by an explicit partitioning algorithm rather than arising organically from genuine differences in the underlying data-generating processes across devices. The second experiment addresses this limitation directly: we instantiate an actual two-client federation using the real Raspberry Pi and gateway devices, each training on its own genuinely collected telemetry without any synthetic partitioning whatsoever, allowing the natural architectural, thermal, and behavioral differences between an ARM64 embedded device and an x86_64 gateway machine to produce whatever heterogeneity they organically produce. This experiment's strength is realism, it is the closest approximation available to us of an actual deployed federated edge system, at the cost of providing only a single heterogeneity condition rather than a sweep, and only two clients rather than a larger simulated population. Read together, the controlled sweep tells us how performance should behave as a function of heterogeneity in principle, while the real-hardware experiment tells us where, on that spectrum, genuinely different edge hardware actually falls and whether its behavior is consistent with the controlled study's predictions.

| | Heterogeneity sweep | Real-hardware federation |
|---|---|---|
| Clients | 10 (simulated, Dirichlet partitions) | 2 (physical: Pi 4 + gateway) |
| Heterogeneity source | Synthetic, controlled (α swept) | Organic (ARM64 vs x86_64) |
| Seeds | 5 per α level | 1 (single deployed run) |
| Conditions tested | 5 (α = 100, 10, 1, 0.5, 0.1) | 1 (real device pairing) |
| Rounds | 20 per run | 20 |

## Results: Heterogeneity Sweep

The central empirical finding of the controlled heterogeneity sweep is a clear, statistically well-separated divergence between federated and centralized training across most of the heterogeneity spectrum we tested, accompanied by a sharply localized reversal at the most extreme heterogeneity level. Across the independent-and-identically-distributed condition through the high-heterogeneity condition, that is, across Dirichlet concentration values of 100, 10, 1, and 0.5, federated training achieves F1 scores ranging from approximately 0.657 to 0.721, consistently and substantially outperforming centralized training's stable reference value of 0.577, with the gap between federated and centralized performance ranging from roughly 0.08 to 0.14 F1 points across this entire range, and with standard deviations across the five random seeds remaining tight, between roughly 0.009 and 0.050, indicating that this advantage is a robust and replicable effect rather than an artifact of any single favorable random initialization. Local-only training, by contrast, performs poorly throughout this entire range, with F1 scores between approximately 0.17 and 0.25, confirming that some form of collaboration between clients, whether through federation or centralization, is necessary to achieve reasonable detection performance on this task, and that federation specifically extracts substantially more value from that collaboration than centralization does, presumably because each client's local model retains some degree of specialization to its own data distribution even as it benefits from globally averaged information, whereas centralized training must find a single set of parameters that performs adequately across all clients' distributions simultaneously without any such specialization. The picture changes sharply at the most extreme heterogeneity level we tested, where Dirichlet concentration equals 0.1 and each simulated client's data is overwhelmingly dominated by a single class: here federated training's F1 score collapses to approximately 0.352, now below centralized training's stable 0.577, with a markedly elevated standard deviation of approximately 0.277 indicating substantial instability across random seeds at this extreme setting, consistent with the theoretical expectation that naive weighted averaging of highly divergent local updates produces an unreliable global model under conditions of severe client drift. AUROC results across the same conditions follow a qualitatively similar but somewhat less dramatic pattern, with federated training maintaining an advantage over centralized training across nearly the entire heterogeneity spectrum including, notably, at the most extreme setting, suggesting that while federated averaging substantially degrades the model's ability to make confident, well-calibrated binary predictions under extreme heterogeneity, as captured by F1 score, it retains comparatively more of its ability to rank anomalous samples above normal ones, as captured by AUROC, even in this degraded regime.

## Real-Hardware Validation

Beyond the controlled, simulated heterogeneity sweep, we validate our central findings using an actual two-client federation deployed across the real Raspberry Pi 4 and gateway hardware described in the Method and Dataset sections, with each device training exclusively on its own genuinely collected telemetry and no synthetic partitioning of any kind. On this real-hardware deployment, federated training achieves an F1 score of 0.937 and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.991, substantially outperforming every alternative we measured: local-only training on the Raspberry Pi alone achieves an F1 score of 0.819 with AUROC of 0.893, local-only training on the gateway device alone achieves a lower F1 score of 0.698 despite a comparable AUROC of 0.918, and centralized training in which both devices' data is pooled into a single model achieves the weakest performance of all three alternatives, with an F1 score of just 0.638 and AUROC of 0.857. This ordering is consistent with, and indeed more pronounced than, the pattern observed in the controlled simulation sweep at moderate-to-mild heterogeneity levels, supporting the interpretation that the real ARM64-versus-x86_64 hardware pairing falls within the realistic-to-moderate region of the heterogeneity spectrum where federation provides its largest advantage, rather than anywhere near the extreme-heterogeneity regime where our controlled sweep predicts federation would underperform centralization. The fact that centralized training underperforms even simple local-only training on the stronger of the two individual devices is itself a notable and somewhat counterintuitive finding: it indicates that naively pooling telemetry from architecturally dissimilar devices without any device-specific adaptation can actively harm model performance relative to training on a single device's data in isolation, because the pooled model must compromise between two systematically different feature distributions rather than specializing to either, whereas federated averaging allows each device to retain a degree of local specialization between communication rounds while still benefiting from shared global structure. The total communication cost of the federated training run across all twenty rounds is approximately 290 kilobytes, equivalent to roughly 14.5 kilobytes transmitted per round, a figure substantially smaller than a single typical JPEG photograph and well within the data budget of a single 2G or EDGE-class mobile session, directly supporting the practical claim that federated anomaly detection of this kind is deployable in genuinely bandwidth-constrained edge environments without requiring any infrastructure upgrade beyond the minimal connectivity already assumed to be available.

## Discussion

The combined results from the controlled heterogeneity sweep and the real-hardware validation experiment support a coherent and, we believe, practically useful account of when and why federated learning should be expected to outperform its alternatives in low-compute edge anomaly detection settings. Under realistic-to-moderate data heterogeneity, the regime we believe most genuinely deployed edge fleets will actually occupy, in which devices differ meaningfully in their typical operating conditions and workload patterns but are not adversarially or pathologically dissimilar, federated averaging is able to aggregate complementary information that no single client could learn from its own data alone, while still preserving enough device-specific specialization between communication rounds to avoid the compromises that centralized pooling is forced to make. This explains both why federation outperforms local-only training, which forgoes the benefit of any cross-device information sharing whatsoever, and why it outperforms centralized training, which must find parameters that work reasonably well across all devices' distributions simultaneously rather than allowing any device-specific adaptation. The breakdown we observe at the most extreme heterogeneity level tested in the controlled sweep is not, in our view, a flaw to be explained away but rather a genuine and theoretically expected limitation of the standard FedAvg algorithm specifically, rather than of federated learning as a general paradigm: when individual clients' local data distributions are sufficiently divergent, in the extreme case, when each client sees almost exclusively one class, their locally optimal model updates point in substantially different directions in parameter space, and naive averaging of these divergent updates, the operation at the heart of FedAvg, produces a global model that may be a poor compromise rather than a genuine synthesis. This phenomenon, generally termed client drift in the federated learning literature, motivated the development of more sophisticated aggregation algorithms such as FedProx, which adds a proximal regularization term penalizing local updates that drift too far from the global model, and SCAFFOLD, which uses control variates to explicitly correct for the variance introduced by heterogeneous local updates; evaluating whether either of these drift-corrected methods recovers federation's advantage in the extreme-heterogeneity regime where standard FedAvg fails is a natural and well-motivated direction for follow-up work building directly on the operating envelope characterized here. Importantly, the consistency between our controlled simulation and our real-hardware deployment, in which genuine ARM64-versus-x86_64 architectural differences produce heterogeneity that falls within the regime where federation helps, rather than the extreme regime where it would hurt, provides meaningful evidence that the crossover phenomenon we characterize is not merely an artifact of our particular synthetic partitioning scheme, but reflects a real property of how federated averaging behaves under genuinely different data-generating processes, which is the central methodological contribution we believe this work offers beyond the existing literature on federated edge anomaly detection.

## Conclusion

This work set out to answer a question that is frequently assumed rather than directly tested in the federated learning literature applied to edge anomaly detection: under what conditions does federation actually outperform its simpler alternatives, and does this hold when heterogeneity arises from genuinely different hardware rather than synthetic data partitioning. Through a combination of a controlled, statistically rigorous heterogeneity sweep spanning ten simulated clients and five random seeds, and a real-hardware deployment across an ARM64 Raspberry Pi 4 and an x86_64 gateway machine, we find that federated learning provides substantial and consistent benefit over both centralized and local-only training across the realistic-to-high heterogeneity regime, achieving an F1 score of 0.937 and AUROC of 0.991 on real hardware at a total communication cost of approximately 290 kilobytes across the full twenty-round training process, a figure comfortably within the budget of a single constrained 2G or EDGE mobile data session, directly supporting the practical viability of this approach in genuinely bandwidth-limited edge deployments. We additionally characterize a principled and theoretically expected failure mode at the extreme end of the heterogeneity spectrum, where standard federated averaging is outperformed by simple centralized pooling due to client drift, providing edge-system designers with a concrete, evidence-based sense of the operating envelope within which federation can be trusted to help rather than inadvertently harm model performance. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies in this specific application area to localize this crossover point using telemetry collected from genuinely heterogeneous instruction-set architectures, complementing the substantial existing body of work demonstrating federated learning's general feasibility on homogeneous edge device fleets or under synthetically partitioned heterogeneity, and we hope it offers a useful, concretely quantified reference point for practitioners deciding whether federated training is the appropriate architectural choice for their own particular low-compute, bandwidth-constrained deployment.

---

## Limitations

Several limitations of this work should be stated plainly, both to properly scope our claims and to motivate the most natural directions for future research building on these results. First and most significantly, our real-hardware validation involves only two physical devices; while this is sufficient to demonstrate that genuine architectural heterogeneity produces effects consistent with our controlled simulation, it does not by itself constitute the larger-scale federated deployment that a production edge fleet would actually involve, and the ten-client population used in our controlled sweep to satisfy methodological expectations around federated learning scale is, by necessity, synthetically partitioned from the same underlying two-device dataset rather than independently collected from ten genuinely distinct physical devices. Second, our heterogeneity sweep, while spanning a wide range from near-identical to extreme distributions, necessarily samples this continuous spectrum at only five discrete points, and our most extreme heterogeneity condition in particular, where federated training's standard deviation across random seeds reaches approximately 0.277, would benefit from additional random seeds beyond the five used here to more precisely characterize both the central tendency and the substantial variance we observe in this unstable regime. Third, this work addresses a single task domain, namely binary anomaly detection on tabular system-telemetry features; while we believe the underlying heterogeneity-crossover phenomenon we characterize is likely to generalize to other edge machine learning tasks, including image or video-based anomaly detection relevant to surveillance applications, we have not empirically tested this generalization, and the specific numerical crossover point we identify is almost certainly task- and feature-dependent rather than a universal constant. Fourth, and following directly from our discussion of client drift, we have evaluated only the standard FedAvg aggregation algorithm and have not yet tested whether drift-corrected alternatives such as FedProx or SCAFFOLD are able to recover federation's advantage in the extreme-heterogeneity regime where standard averaging fails, which would be a natural and valuable extension demonstrating whether the crossover we observe is a fundamental limitation of federation as a paradigm or merely a limitation of the specific, simplest aggregation algorithm we tested. Finally, our experiments measure model accuracy and communication cost but do not directly measure end-to-end deployment factors such as device battery consumption under sustained federated training, behavior under intermittent or lossy network conditions rather than the idealized reliable communication assumed in our protocol, or robustness to adversarial or malfunctioning clients, each of which would be relevant to a fully production-ready deployment of this system.
